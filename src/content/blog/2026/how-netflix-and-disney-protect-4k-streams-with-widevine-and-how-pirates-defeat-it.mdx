## Punished for watching legally

Not _too_ long ago, I switched to Mac from Windows after my custom-built PC died and I couldn't be bothered to build another one. I had a MacBook Pro from a previous job and a Thunderbolt dock for my keyboard, mouse, and two monitors.

The setup worked fine, until I tried to watch anything on Netflix or Amazon Prime Video.
I could hear the audio and see the subtitles, but the video was just a black screen.

<Image
  src={NetflixBlackScreen}
  alt="Netflix showing a black screen with audio playing, due to DRM restrictions when using a DisplayLink dock."
/>

The Thunderbolt dock I was using connected to the MBP with a single USB-C cable, and from there I connected two monitors via HDMI. I would say, an extremely average setup for a docked laptop.

The problem was that this dock uses [DisplayLink](https://www.displaylink.com/) to emulate multiple monitors.
To do that, it has to make use of Apple's Screen Capture API. If you use a dock like this, you might notice the little icon in the menu bar showing your screen is currently being captured.

Well, streaming platforms use <WikiLink url="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_rights_management" tooltip="Digital rights management uses technology to control access to copyrighted works.">Digital Rights Management</WikiLink> (DRM)
to control where and how their content is played back. In this case, the video is decrypted in a protected area that is inaccessible to the Screen Capture API.

So ironically, it's very easy to be punished for attempting to watch content legally, where for example, if you were to pirate the same content, DRM-free, you would have no problem watching it on your docked MacBook Pro.

This started getting me really interested in _how_ a website like Netflix was able to prevent me from taking a screenshot on my own computer.
How is it that pirates are able to defeat DRM and create WEB-DL and WEBRip releases, while I can't even watch it on my own computer?

## The Gatekeepers: DRM Systems

<Image
  src={DRMSystems}
  alt="Diagram illustrating the DRM systems used by major streaming platforms."
/>

Each streaming platform only enables viewing of their content on a limited number of licensed devices that have been certified for secure playback using one of these systems, depending on the device and application.

The most common players in the space are [**Google's Widevine**](https://www.widevine.com/), [**Apple's FairPlay**](https://developer.apple.com/streaming/fps/), and [**Microsoft's PlayReady**](https://www.microsoft.com/playready/).
Widevine is a particularly interesting target for hackers and pirates, because it is used in a massive variety of environments, some more secure than others.

In order to playback content at the highest resolution, the playing device must have a <WikiLink url="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trusted_execution_environment" tooltip="A Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) is a secure area of a main processor.">Trusted Execution Environment (TEE)</WikiLink>,
inaccessible to any user, where a device-specific decryption key is stored. We'll be getting back to that too.

On rare occasion, a manufacturer may make a mistake and ship a device that accidentally (or lazily) exposes that key, one way or another.

<Image
  src={WidevinePartners}
  alt="Diagram showing the various partners and devices that support Google's Widevine DRM system. For example, Widevine is used by Netflix, HBO Max, Disney+, Paramount+, Amazon Prime Video, and more. It's also used on Android devices, Google Chrome, PlayStation, Roku, Tesla, and more."
/>

These platforms would strongly prefer users watch their content on one of these locked down devices,
where decryption keys are safe from the owner.
On devices that offer more user control, like most Linux computers, you'll notice your streaming options are usually fairly limited.

## Different Rules for Different Devices

If you look at the [system requirements for Netflix](https://help.netflix.com/en/node/14361), you can start to get a hint for how they manage access between different classes of devices.

Notice, if you are on a Mac, the only way to watch Netflix in 4K is through the Safari browser. Google Chrome will only let you watch up to 1080p.

On a Linux machine, you can only watch up to 720p no matter what (actually _while_ writing this, they appear to have quietly raised Linux to 1080p. That will warrant some exploring).

Notice too, Netflix only supports back to a certain version of each browser, ensuring any vulnerabilities that might allow a user to bypass DRM, are somewhat short-lived.
On Android, HD playback is restricted to _the latest version of Android OS_, fairly restrictive.

## How Streaming Platforms Encrypt Their Content

To watch your favorite TV show or movie on a streaming platform, it first starts with a studio master, the highest possible quality version of the media.

The studio master, or mezzanine, is a _massive_ lossless (or near-lossless) file, often hundreds of gigabytes in size.
This is what studios use to create Blu-ray copies, and more commonly now, what is sent to streaming platforms to be encoded, encrypted, and streamed to paying customers.

<Image
  src={StreamingServiceDRMArchitecture}
  alt="Diagram showing a streaming service encoding a studio master into multiple resolutions, encrypting DASH media segments, obtaining Widevine license keys, and serving encrypted media through a CDN."
/>

### Encoding the Studio Master

The streaming provider will first encode the studio master into a variety of resolutions and bitrates to accommodate a range of devices, network conditions, and user plans.

These encoded versions are ultimately what you will watch, but you aren't going to watch them directly.
They aren't optimized for streaming, and more importantly to the studios, they are currently unprotected.

The pirate's goal is to gain access to these encodes, aiming for 1080p or 4K, but the next few steps are what makes that challenging.

### Packaging for Streaming with Shaka Packager

To stream reliably, video must be packaged into a streaming format such as [HLS](https://developer.apple.com/streaming/) or [DASH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_Adaptive_Streaming_over_HTTP).

[Shaka Packager](https://github.com/google/shaka-packager) is one popular open-source tool that does this, by splitting encoded media into segments, preparing them for adaptive playback, _and optionally encrypting the video_ for DRM such as <WikiLink url="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widevine" tooltip="Widevine is a digital rights management (DRM) technology used by streaming services to protect video content.">Widevine</WikiLink>.

For DASH, it produces a "Media Presentation", a collection of media files and a manifest (MPD) that describes the available tracks, their bitrates, and how to access them.

If the content is protected, such as with Widevine, Shaka Packager will request a content encryption key (**CEK**), and its corresponding key ID (**KID**) from the Widevine License Server (which in-turn will get it from the KMS), and use that key to encrypt the media segments using [Common Encryption (CENC)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Encryption) with AES-128 in CTR mode.
This is a symmetric encryption algorithm, meaning the same key is used to encrypt and decrypt the content.

When the subscriber wants to watch the content, the video player will request a license from the Widevine License Server, which will return the CEK for that KID if the user is authorized to view it.

#### The Manifest (MPD)

The Manifest (MPD) file is like a table of contents for the stream.
Your video player will first download the MPD, and then decide which segments to start downloading and playing based on the device, network conditions, and license.
This is also how your phone is able to switch between 1080p and 720p mid-stream, without interrupting playback.

For protected streams, the MPD also contains some additional metadata that tells the player which tracks are protected, and how to obtain a license for them.

Shaka Packager identifies the protected track and its KID in a `ContentProtection` element, and generates a **Protection System Specific Header (PSSH)** for Widevine.
The PSSH is DRM-specific initialization data, not the CEK itself.

When playback begins, the browser's Widevine CDM uses that signaling to create a license challenge. The video application sends the challenge to the Widevine License Server, which authorizes the viewer and returns a license for the matching content key.

The license service can use the KID to resolve the corresponding CEK through its key-management system. The CDM uses that key internally to decrypt the `cenc` segments; the JavaScript player does not receive the raw CEK.
